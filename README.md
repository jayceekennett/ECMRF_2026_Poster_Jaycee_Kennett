# Cognitive Representations of Self-Harm as Predictors of Coping Orientation and Attitudes Towards Service Engagement

Supplementary information for poster presented at ECMRF 2026
This repository accompanies a poster presented at
10th Suicide & Self-Harm Early and Mid-Career Researchers’ Forum, 2026.

## Abstract

How individuals understand their own self-harm is a crucial aspect of lived experience, but how their individual cognitive representations affect other outcomes is unclear. As such, this study examined whether cognitive representations of self-harm predict coping styles and attitudes towards seeking mental health support.

A cross-sectional online survey design with 113 participants was used to assess associations between self-reported subscales of the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (B-IPQ) and outcomes from the Brief COPE (B-COPE) scale and Inventory of Attitudes Towards Seeking Mental Health Services (IASMHS). Data was collected between October 2021 and October 2022. Multiple linear regression models were constructed with B-IPQ variables as predictors.

All regression models were significant with adjusted R2 values ranging from 0.051 to 0.216 and multiple B-IPQ variables were significant predictors. Negative coping styles (self-blame, denial, reduced venting, and reduced problem-focussed coping) were predicted by greater perceived consequences of one’s own self-harm and expectations of engaging in it for longer. Whereas the perception of insight into self-harm and having control over it predicted multiple positive outcomes including lower denial, greater use of support, and better help-seeking attitudes.

These findings highlight that not only how an individual understands their self-harm is important, but that these cognitive representations represent a clinically meaningful aspect of lived experience. This has the potential to inform clinical communication, the design of psychoeducational materials, and how self-harm is framed across policy and diagnostic contexts.
